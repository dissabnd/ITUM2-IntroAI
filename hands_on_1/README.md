## Industrial Problem & Learning Objectives
A rubber products plant is developing a carbon-black-filled natural rubber (NR) compound. Every lab trial takes a day (mill mixing, MDR, press cure, conditioning, tensile test). The lab has 120 past trials. Can a model learn which recipe gives the best tensile strength before the next batch is mixed?

In this lab, students:
1. Load 120 lab trials (`rubber_compound_lab_data.csv`).
2. Plot the data and see that tensile strength has an **optimum** in sulphur, while hardness rises almost linearly with carbon black.
3. Train a **linear regression** and a **Random Forest** to predict tensile strength, and compare them.
4. Check the scores with **5-fold cross-validation**.
5. Compare what drives **tensile strength, hardness and t90**.
6. Use the model as a formulator (sulphur sweep), then test it **outside the trial range**.

## 💡 Facilitator Answers (seed = 7)
1. **Q2: optimum in sulphur.** Tensile rises to about 1.5 phr sulphur and then falls. A tighter network restricts chain mobility and strain-induced crystallisation, so the rubber becomes stiffer but more brittle. Hardness, by contrast, rises close to linearly with black (≈ 1 Shore A per 2 phr).
2. **Step 3.** On the 24-trial test split: linear R² = 0.74 (MAE 1.21 MPa), Random Forest R² = 0.82 (MAE 0.98 MPa).
3. **Q4.** 5-fold CV gives linear **0.41 ± 0.16**, Random Forest **0.66 ± 0.15**. Both are lower than the single split; report the CV figures. The fold-to-fold spread (one fold falls to 0.09 / 0.36) shows how much a small test set can flatter.
4. **Q5.** t90 is driven by **cure temperature** (importance ≈ 0.85), with CBS second: temperature sets the cure **rate**. Tensile and hardness are driven by **black and sulphur**: the recipe sets the **properties**. For hardness, linear CV R² = **0.98** vs. Random Forest **0.93**: when the physics is close to linear, the simple model wins and is easier to explain.
5. **Step 6.** The Random Forest sweep peaks at **≈ 1.5 phr → 26.1 MPa**. The linear model gives a slope of about −0.05 MPa per phr: it concludes that sulphur does not matter, because the rise and fall cancel in a straight-line fit. Importance from a linear model would mislead the formulator.
6. **Q6. Extrapolation.** At 5.0 phr the Random Forest returns **21.5 MPa**, the same as at its edge (3.0 phr). Tree models flat-line outside the data. The trend says a 5 phr compound would be weaker still (and more prone to sulphur bloom). Run a lab batch.
7. **Leakage.** t90, ΔTorque and hardness are measured after curing, so they are excluded.
